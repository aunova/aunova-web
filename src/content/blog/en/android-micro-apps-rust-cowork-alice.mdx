On 5 November 2026 two small Android apps went live on Google Play under Aunova OÜ. ModelMeter tells you which local AI models your phone can run. BluBelt is a training log for martial artists. Neither app is big, and that is the point. What we want to show is how they got there: one Rust codebase shape, one reusable template, a team of AI agents doing much of the building and checking, and a human who signs off on everything that goes out.

This post walks through all of it, including the parts that did not go to plan.

**Try them on Google Play:** [ModelMeter](https://play.google.com/store/apps/details?id=com.aunova.modelmeter) and [BluBelt](https://play.google.com/store/apps/details?id=com.aunova.blubelt). Both are free.

## Two apps, one shape

ModelMeter reads your phone's hardware and lists which local AI models fit on it. It shows the math rather than a bare verdict: the fit check uses total RAM, and each model's detail page spells out how the number was reached, so you can check our reasoning instead of taking it on trust.

## The stack

Each app is written in Rust, with the UI in Slint, and packaged for Android with Gradle. A small Java shim bridges Rust to Google's SDKs through JNI: Google Mobile Ads with Google's consent tool (UMP), and Play Billing. R8 shrinks the Java bridge. Builds target arm64, with 16 KB page alignment and target SDK 36.

The reason for this shape is reuse. The shared parts live in starter crates that every new app pulls in, and the platform-specific surface stays down to one small shim.

That shim is also where the stack bites. Rust calls Java methods by name, and R8, doing its job, renames or removes code it believes nobody uses. Left alone, that produces an app that builds cleanly and breaks the first time Rust reaches across the bridge. So a JNI gate refuses a release build if R8 renamed or removed anything Rust calls by name. The failure moves from a user's phone to our build.

## The template

The apps share a reusable micro-app template: a generator script, starter crates, and a playbook. The playbook holds more than 80 recorded traps, and each one names the guard or test that now prevents it.

The guards are not advice. A verifier refuses a release bundle on any of 22 checks. Some examples: test ad ids still present, the wrong signing key, missing 16 KB alignment, a permission list that differs from the live privacy page, a missing ad-measurement delay. Every guard was fed its bad input once, so we could watch it refuse. A guard that has never said no is a guess.

Here is the honest number. BluBelt, the second app, went from generated project to Google Play internal testing in 26 hours 37 minutes of wall-clock time. Our target was one day, and we missed it. The overrun was QA rounds, not template steps. That distinction matters to us: the template did its part, and the extra hours went into checking the work.

## How Cowork:Alice runs a release

Cowork:Alice is Aunova's multi-agent company, running on the omp coding harness. Alice is the orchestrator: she plans the work and delegates it to department heads, each with a defined area. Linus leads code, Ferris is the Rust specialist, Vera handles compliance, and there are others.

Karl is the one we would point to first. He is an adversarial reviewer who assumes the work is broken and verifies by acting. He runs the tests, kills mutants to make sure the tests can fail at all, and re-derives numbers instead of trusting the ones he was handed. His job is to turn "it works" from something stated into something shown.

Then come the owner's gates. Agents draft; the owner approves anything public, any payment, and every store submission. Decisions about the business stay with the human. The agents do a great deal of the work between those gates, the building, reviewing and checking, but nothing goes to Google Play, and no post like this one goes out, without a person saying yes.

## Continuous learning, told straight

Every mistake goes to two places. It goes into the template's playbook as a trap, with the guard that prevents it. And it goes into a shared knowledge base that the agents search before they start work, so the next agent meets the lesson before it meets the problem.

We also keep a ledger of which behavioural fixes worked, and its finding is the most useful thing this whole effort has taught us. Mechanical guards and tests are the fixes measured as working. Written rules on their own, a sentence added to an agent's instructions, have never been measured as working. So wherever a lesson can become a guard, it does. A check that the build enforces does not depend on anyone remembering it.

A current example, with its status stated plainly. A recent release round ran for 12 hours. Out of it came a new rule (fix the scope before work starts, and build right after the reviewer passes) and changes to the agent definitions. It is still untested. The next release is where it gets measured, and the result goes into the ledger either way.

## Privacy and consent

No ad without consent. Ads appear only while the Privacy options button is shown, so anyone who sees an ad has a way to withdraw. Google's ad measurement waits for consent too, and the release verifier refuses a bundle that is missing that delay. Each app has its own privacy notice, for [ModelMeter](/en/privacy/modelmeter) and for [BluBelt](/en/privacy/blubelt), and the same verifier checks that the app's permission list matches what the live notice says.

## What's next

The template exists so that the next app starts where BluBelt ended, with every recorded trap already guarded. The next release is also the first measurement of the new scope rule. If it helps, the ledger will show it. If it does not, that gets recorded too, and the lesson goes looking for a guard instead.
